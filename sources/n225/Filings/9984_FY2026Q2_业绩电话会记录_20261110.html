On November 11, 2026, we revised our earnings presentation material and investor
brieﬁng presentation material for Q2 FY2026 regarding power generation-related
equipment in the U.S. On November 12, we published an updated information to reﬂect
developments following our earnings announcement. This Q&A is based on the
materials published as of November 10, the day of the earnings announcement. For the
latest information, please refer to the revised presentations and the updated
information.

                   SoftBank Group Corp.: Q2 FY2026 Global Conference Call Q&A


November 10, 2026
Presenters:
Yoshimitsu Goto, Board Director, Corporate Oﬃcer, Senior Vice President, CFO & CISO, Head of
      Finance Unit & Administration Unit

Q&A


Speaker 1
Q1:
I have a question about the losses on the power generation-related equipment. In the earnings
presentation, you outlined investments of $9.5 billion and said you expect to recoup it. Will you
recoup the full amount, or the amount after deducting the loss recognized?


A1:
(Goto) Of the $9.5 billion, we plan to recoup the $6.6 billion remaining after deducting the loss
through project ﬁnancing, primarily from the PORTS-Pike power generation project.


Speaker 2
Q1:
At the investor brieﬁng, you mentioned that you intend to smooth out the debt maturity proﬁle by
taking ﬂexible action, including bond buybacks. Bond prices have fallen signiﬁcantly. In particular, the
U.S. dollar-denominated perpetual subordinated bonds with a 6.875% coupon are trading well below

                                                   1
par. Given that you already secured reﬁnancing through the issuance of domestic hybrid bonds in
June this year, and that only a matter of months remains until the ﬁrst call date in July 2027, would it
not make sense to buy back these bonds? Funding costs have also risen overall. Beyond this particular
hybrid bond, I would think that, for bonds maturing within two years and trading below par, you
should use cash for buybacks rather than hold it. Could you share your thoughts?


A1:
(Goto) We are, of course, considering actions based on those scenarios, and there are initiatives
already under way. Beyond the near term, we also have signiﬁcant concentrations of maturities three
to ﬁve years from now. We believe actions such as buybacks are particularly necessary to address
those concentrations. As for the near term, I think your suggestion makes a great deal of sense.


Q2:
You have made cumulative advance payments of approximately $9.5 billion for power generation-
related equipment for AI data centers in the U.S., eﬀectively as bridge investments. Looking ahead,
and not just in the U.S., to what extent and over what timeframe should we expect this type of
funding support through bridge investments and advance payments to increase? I understand that
these investments will ultimately be reﬁnanced through project ﬁnancing, leaving you responsible
only for the equity portion. However, if project ﬁnancing is generally arranged after data center
construction is completed, is it correct to understand that you would continue to bear the funding
burden for a considerable period, perhaps around two years?


A2:
(Goto) This bridge investment diﬀers somewhat from a typical transaction. In this case, developments
at other companies meant that we needed to move quickly with the equipment construction plans.
Going forward, we would prefer, as far as possible, for ﬁnancing to be arranged entirely at the
individual project level. We consider that the standard approach. That said, we currently have
suﬃcient ﬁnancial capacity, so we may provide funding as temporary support for individual projects.
This diﬀers somewhat from the usual role of an investment company, and we would prefer to avoid it
where possible. However, there are various ways to enhance SBG’s corporate value. Rather than
limiting ourselves to a single approach, we believe it is appropriate to pursue a range of initiatives to
support our growth. From a ﬁnancial perspective, however, maintaining our ﬁnancial policy is our
highest priority. Provided we adhere to that policy, there may be occasions when we can respond to a
project’s short-term funding needs, for example, when we have ample headroom in terms of LTV and
liquidity.

Could you remind us of the status of the Texas digital infrastructure sites with respect to pending
permits? How do you see the regulatory backdrop following the election? Is it aﬀecting your strategy

Speaker 4
Q1:
You reported a loan to value of 15.9% at the end of September. Could you provide an update? Stock
prices, particularly Arm's, have moved since then. Where would LTV stand if you calculated it today?
How much of your NAV is currently accounted for by Arm and OpenAI, and what valuation is used for
the OpenAI stake?


A1:
(Goto) As you noted, market declines since the end of September have reduced our NAV, and our
current LTV is higher than 15.9%. We do not disclose daily LTV ﬁgures. Regarding reports of a new
funding round at OpenAI, we are not in a position to comment. If a round takes place, we would
reassess our valuation of OpenAI. If that results in a higher valuation, it would have a positive impact
on NAV and LTV. We will take various measures toward the end of December to maintain LTV at a safe

                                                   3
level. Asset-backed ﬁnancing and asset monetization are among the options. Slowing the pace of
investment or refraining from new investments can be even more eﬀective and are also important. In
any event, we are managing LTV at a safe level.


Q2:
As you manage these ﬂuctuations, what upper limit would you like to maintain for loan to value in the
near term?


A2:
(Goto) Our ﬁnancial policy is to maintain LTV below 25%. In general ﬁnancial management terms, 25%
is a very safe level. It does not mean that exceeding 25% would be dangerous. We are supported not
only by professional investors but also by many retail shareholders and bondholders. We have set
25% as a guideline for LTV because we want our policy to be both easy for those investors to
understand and ﬁnancially prudent. We intend to maintain stable ﬁnancial management within that
range.


(Ohama) You also asked about Arm's share. At the end of September, Arm accounted for 54% of the
equity value of our holdings. This is shown in the earnings presentation.


Q3:
And OpenAI?


A3:
(Ohama) The 54% ﬁgure was for Arm. OpenAI accounted for 21% at the end of September. These
ﬁgures are in the earnings presentation material on our website.


Speaker 5
Q1:
I would like to better understand the revision to the procurement plans for power generation-related
equipment. Could you explain a little more, for example, whether you changed the type of equipment
to be used or whether the plans themselves have been delayed somewhat? Without further
information, we could form the wrong impression, so I would appreciate some clariﬁcation.


A1:
(Goto) When starting large-scale data center projects, there was a possibility that data center
operators would compete to secure critical equipment and supplies with very long lead times. We

                                                 4
therefore placed orders early for the equipment needed to move these projects forward. We believed
this would give our project partners and others greater conﬁdence in proceeding. As we began work,
it became clear that equipment requirements varied from project to project and that a uniform
approach would not work. Through those discussions, we found that we needed to review the
relevant contracts individually in light of each project’s speciﬁc requirements. We would prefer not to
comment on the details of the equipment and supplies, as these are strategic matters. Broadly
speaking, as we took concrete steps and reviewed our plans along the way, we needed to determine
what to retain and what to discontinue.


Q2:
Is it fair to understand that there were some diﬀerences between the speciﬁcations originally
envisaged and those of the equipment ordered?


A2:
(Goto) I think that was one of the factors. I should add that PORTS-Pike is progressing very well and is
not included in this loss.


Speaker 6

You disclosed an initial investment of $1 billion in the Malaysian project. I think you previously said
that such projects would be funded with 20–30% equity and 70–80% debt. Is the initial $1 billion

A1:
(Goto) An initial $1.85 billion, including our $1 billion contribution, is planned to be invested in the
project as equity. We expect the project to use that equity to raise debt ﬁnancing. However, the

through announcements and other disclosures, on how the speciﬁc scale of the project will be
determined. We would appreciate a little more time.

Is the additional $4.2 billion in asset-backed ﬁnancing at SVF2 guaranteed by the holding company? Is
there room to obtain more asset-backed ﬁnancing at SVF2?

Speaker 7
Q1:
Given the strong demand for power equipment, I would have expected the contracts to retain some
value even if SBG no longer needed the equipment. Is there a reason the contracts could not be
transferred to another buyer?


A1:
(Goto) The speciﬁcations of power generation-related equipment depend on each project’s
requirements, including the required generation capacity, site conditions, and the voltage and
frequency of the power grid. It is therefore not simply a matter of selling the equipment.
[Supplementary information: In addition, many of the items for which losses were recognized
involved advance payments made to secure manufacturing capacity and purchase equipment, making
these payments diﬃcult to recover through refunds or transfers to third parties.]


Speaker 8
Q1:
Reports in the Financial Times and elsewhere describe this as a loss on data centers, but I believe it
actually relates to power generation. I had understood that SBG acted as a prime contractor or
project manager in the power generation business and did not bear this type of equipment risk. Does
your role as project manager also involve taking on the risks and responsibilities associated with
procuring and using the equipment?


A1:
(Goto) I do not think the media’s description of this as a data center issue is quite appropriate. With
regard to power generation, as you said, our role is to exercise proper oversight. We considered it
urgent to procure certain equipment because, unless we secured it early, we would have to wait in
line. We could not aﬀord to wait until project ﬁnancing was arranged. Given our NAV of approximately
¥60 trillion at the end of September and our credit strength, we judged that the level of risk was well
within our capacity to absorb, and proceeded with procurement. At the same time, we need to use
the most suitable equipment as each project’s requirements change or become more speciﬁc. As a
result, some of the equipment procured may not ﬁt those requirements. It may be suitable for other
projects, but for accounting purposes we need to assess its value as an asset appropriately at the
current point in time. We therefore made a conservative assessment at the end of this quarter and
recognized a loss. We would like to reduce the number of such cases going forward. Depending on

                                                  6
the circumstances, however, there may still be investments that require us to act quickly. In those
cases, we intend to apply the lessons from this experience and proceed in a way that minimizes losses
as far as possible.


Q2:
The government has announced a total investment of approximately $33 billion for the gas-ﬁred
power plants under the Japan-U.S. project. Meanwhile, SBG has disclosed cumulative payments of
$9.5 billion since FY2025. Is it correct to understand that approximately 30% of the investment has
been made on a payment basis?


A2:
(Goto) Of the $9.5 billion, at least the $5.6 billion relating to PORTS-Pike falls within that scope. We
expect to make investments in stages in line with the amount announced by the government.
However, while the project is still under way, it is diﬃcult to provide a precise reconciliation between
the payments we have made and the total investment amount.


Q3:
In this earnings announcement, you indicated that cumulative revenue from Arm AGI CPU in FY2026
and FY2027 could reach up to $2 billion, and that you intend to review the revenue outlook around
spring 2027. At that time, will you also revise the $15 billion revenue outlook for FY2030?


A3:
(Thornton) At last week’s results, we announced that we had secured purchase orders from multiple
customers and that our conﬁdence in achieving up to $2 billion in cumulative revenue had increased.
We also entered into supply agreements with suppliers to secure the chips needed to fulﬁll those
customer orders. Ninety days ago, we were hopeful of achieving more than $1 billion. We now think
we can reach up to $2 billion. However, both we and our suppliers still have a lot of work to do to
execute those contracts. We intend to provide quarterly updates, with the next at our Q3 results in
February and another in May, so we will keep you informed of progress. We have not yet revisited the
$15 billion ﬁgure for FY2030. We expect to revisit that ﬁgure at our next analyst event, currently
planned for next spring. That will be the next opportunity to review that number.


Q4:
Arm previously estimated the 2030 TAM for data center CPUs at $100 billion. Consumer inference
demand, such as Meta’s Muse, is also emerging, whereas I believe your previous estimate may have
covered only the enterprise TAM. Leaving aside speciﬁc ﬁgures, will you include the consumer

                                                    7
inference market in your TAM calculations going forward?


A4:
(Thornton) At our last Analyst Day in March, we said we thought the data center CPU TAM would be
$100 billion plus. We knew the “plus” was doing a lot of work, but left the potential market size open
beyond saying it would exceed $100 billion. Since then, I think NVIDIA has said $200 billion, AMD has
said $220 billion. The market could potentially be much larger. However, we have not yet redone the
calculations to provide our view. We will do that ahead of our next Analyst Day next spring.


Speaker 9
Q1:
I think you mentioned potential bond buybacks to manage LTV at this morning's investor brieﬁng, but
I'm not sure I understood correctly. Could you conﬁrm that and explain how you view buybacks as a
tool to manage LTV?


A1:
(Goto) Bond buybacks serve several purposes. For example, some of our bonds are currently trading at
quite low prices, and we believe buying them back can make economic sense. As I mentioned earlier,
conducting buybacks where needed to manage our maturity proﬁle is another important strategic
objective. The approach will diﬀer from company to company, but I believe both are valid reasons.

A1:

loans. When the loans were repaid early, approximately ¥100 billion of costs relating to their
remaining terms was recognized in interest expense in a lump sum. I believe this was one of the
factors behind the higher interest expense.

A5:

But isn't SB Energy the company setting up the data centers?


A6:
(Goto) The loss recognized this time relates to bridge investments we made, before individual

not, and reassessed their values appropriately for accounting purposes. This is an accounting matter

Speaker 12
Q1:
What is your view on share buybacks?


A1:
(Goto) Share buybacks are one of the options we consider on an ongoing basis. There are three broad
uses for the funds we raise. The ﬁrst is to fund our businesses and new investments. The second is
shareholder returns, including share buybacks and dividends. The third is to strengthen our ﬁnancial
position. These are three important uses of funds for us as an investment company. We will consider
the optimal combination and which to prioritize at any given time. Share buybacks remain an
important consideration, but we would prefer not to comment on timing or other details.


Speaker 13
Q1:
I believe both Masayoshi Son and you have frequently discussed the company's stance shifting
between oﬀense and defense modes. Where do you think SBG stands between these two modes
now?


A1:
(Goto) From a medium- to long-term perspective, I believe we should be in oﬀense mode. A declining
market is, in principle, a good time to invest. However, because the overall value of our own asset
holdings also declines, I believe we need to be in defense mode in the short term.

                                                  10
It is a question of how we use the accelerator and the brake. As we steer the company, when we
switch between them and for how long are very important strategic considerations.

Speaker 16
Q1:
As a credit investor, I am pleased to hear you are focusing on defense mode in the short term. What
scale of defensive action should we expect, and how soon? I ask because I think the market is
reassessing valuations of AI-related stocks. S&P's report assumes an Arm share price of $250 to $300,
which is quite diﬀerent from where Arm trades today. How do you view the urgency of taking
defensive action?


A1:
(Goto) There is currently no situation that requires urgent action in our ﬁnancial management.
However, we need to consider every day what is happening now and the potential developments and
risks ahead. In ﬁnancing, we should select and execute transactions that have a positive impact on
LTV. When making investment decisions in an environment like this, it is also important to take some
time to assess the situation. Earlier, the question asked us to choose between oﬀense and defense. In
light of these measures, I said that we were in defense mode in the short term. That does not mean

                                                 11
we have entered defense mode in response to an urgent situation. My point is that it is important to
remain focused on maintaining a safe LTV and to make investment and other decisions with due
regard to the prevailing environment.
